# Stakeholder Mapping: Champion, Economic Buyer, Blocker, User

**Phase 7 · Lesson 02 · ~1h**

## PROBLEM

Six weeks into a pilot at a mid-size logistics company. The demos are landing, the champion — a director of ops — is sending fire-emoji Slack messages after every Wednesday call. Then, out of nowhere, the deal freezes. No renewal signed. It takes the FDE two more weeks to find out why: the CFO, who was never in a single meeting, saw the line item on a budget review and said "who authorized this?" The champion could not answer that question because she never had. The economic buyer had been invisible the entire engagement, and now the FDE has to restart trust-building with someone who first heard about the project through an invoice.

You will build the wrong thing for the wrong person if you cannot see the whole org chart of your engagement.

## INTUITION

Every engagement has four archetypes, and confusing them is the most common failure mode of new FDEs:

- **Champion.** The person who wants you to succeed because your success is *their* success. Usually a director or senior manager. They open doors, get you meetings, defend the project internally. Do not confuse enthusiasm with authority.
- **Economic buyer.** The person who signs the check. Often invisible until the renewal conversation. May not care about the project's substance — they care about ROI, risk, and whether their peers approve.
- **Blocker.** Anyone whose "no" kills the project. Security, legal, IT, a rival VP with a competing initiative, a compliance officer with veto power. Blockers rarely announce themselves.
- **User.** The person whose day changes because of your software. Often *not* the champion, and often skeptical — they've seen three previous initiatives that made their work harder.

The trap: champions are visible, so you optimize for them. Meanwhile the blocker is drafting a memo, the buyer is being told about you by someone else, and the user is muting your Slack channel. A great engagement identifies all four in week one and touches each of them monthly.

## BUILD IT

The **Stakeholder Map Matrix** — one table, updated weekly. Columns:

| Name | Role | Archetype | Influence (1-5) | Support (-2 to +2) | Last touch | Next touch | Ask of them | What they need from us |
|------|------|-----------|-----------------|-----------------------|------------|------------|-------------|------------------------|

Rules for filling it out:

1. **Every named human in the engagement gets a row.** If you have never heard someone's name in six weeks, ask your champion who else is watching.
2. **Archetype can shift.** A user who becomes vocal becomes a champion. A champion who gets promoted becomes a buyer. Re-tag monthly.
3. **Support is not enthusiasm; it is what they would say behind closed doors.** +2 = "we should expand this." 0 = "haven't formed a view." -2 = "we should kill this quietly."
4. **Influence times support = political weight.** A -2 with influence 5 (a hostile CFO) matters ten times more than a +2 with influence 1 (an enthusiastic analyst).

Then add a **Coverage Check** at the bottom:

- Do I know the economic buyer's name? If no, that is the highest-priority task this week.
- Have I met at least one blocker face-to-face? If no, they will surface at the worst moment.
- Have I watched at least one user do the work my software will change? If no, my demo is a fantasy.
- Is my champion still my champion this month? Champions churn — promotions, reorgs, project fatigue.

Optional overlay: a **quadrant chart** with Influence on the y-axis and Support on the x-axis. Manage the top-left (high influence, low support) actively; keep the top-right (high influence, high support) close; you can ignore the bottom-left; convert the bottom-right into advocates.

## FIELD NOTES

- The intern, the executive assistant, and the veteran analyst who has been at the company 22 years know more about the org than any org chart. Get coffee with them within the first month.
- "Champion of one" is a red flag. If only one person is fighting for the project, they will leave, get reorged, or lose interest, and your engagement dies with them. Aim for two champions in different reporting lines by week four.
- The economic buyer often has an "influencer" whispering in their ear — a chief of staff, a trusted VP. Find that person; brief them directly.
- Users become blockers when they hear about the project third-hand. Include them early even if the champion says "we'll loop them in later."
- Reorgs happen roughly every 18 months in large enterprises. Rebuild your map from scratch when one hits.

## INTERVIEW ANGLE

Stakeholder awareness is a core FDE competency and shows up in behavioral rounds at every serious loop. Interviewers listen for whether you distinguish "who was excited" from "who could kill it."

Sample questions:

1. *"Tell me about a project where the loudest supporter wasn't the one who mattered."* — Tests whether you've been burned by champion-versus-buyer confusion. Strong answers name both archetypes explicitly.
2. *"You're three weeks in. Your champion says the CIO 'will be fine with it.' What do you do?"* — Correct instinct: get a meeting with the CIO directly, or at least their delegate. Never let your champion be the sole source of truth about a blocker.
3. *"How would you handle a user who is openly hostile to your project?"* — They want to hear that you'd invest in the user, not route around them. Hostile users become the loudest advocates once converted.

## DRILL

Take an engagement (real or hypothetical) with at least six named people. Fill out the Stakeholder Map Matrix in full. Then answer, in writing: (1) who is your invisible economic buyer and how will you make them visible? (2) which single -2 could kill this project? (3) which +2 with low influence could be groomed into a second champion? Time-box to 30 minutes. Redo the exercise for the same engagement in four weeks and diff the rows — that diff is the story of your political capital.
