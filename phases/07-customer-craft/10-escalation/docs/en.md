# Escalation: When to Burn a Favor, When to Eat the Delay

**Phase 7 · Lesson 10 · ~1h**

## PROBLEM

Week six at a regional utility. The FDE has been waiting eleven days for VPC networking to be provisioned so the pilot can talk to a database. IT is slow but working; the ticket has been touched twice. The champion asks Friday: "should we escalate to my SVP?" The FDE, tired and frustrated, says yes. The champion emails her SVP, who emails the CIO, who emails the network team lead, who — feeling ambushed — pushes the ticket to next week out of principle. The champion looks like she overreacted. The network lead is now cool toward the project. The delay ends up being *worse*, and the FDE has burned trust with the one function whose cooperation he needs for the next six months. Two weeks later there's a real emergency and the champion says "I already used my escalation card, I can't go there again." Now they're actually stuck.

Escalation is a limited resource. Spend it wrong and you have less of it when it matters.

## INTUITION

Every relationship has an escalation budget — the number of times you can go over someone's head before the relationship is permanently damaged. Typically: two per year per person, in most enterprises. Spend them like real capital.

Three questions before every escalation:

1. **Is this actually blocked, or is it just slow?** Slow is expected. Blocked means there is a specific decision or resource nobody can produce at the current level. If you can't name the decision or resource, you're not blocked yet.
2. **Have I tried the direct path exhaustively?** One call, one email, one Slack, one in-person. Escalation before exhausting direct paths tells the escalator you don't do your own work.
3. **Is the pain of the delay greater than the pain of the political cost?** Sometimes the answer is genuinely "eat it." A one-week slip that costs you nothing is cheaper than a burned favor with IT.

The general rule: escalate *for decisions*, not for speed. "We need a decision from your SVP on data-residency scope" is legitimate. "IT is slow on my ticket" almost never is.

## BUILD IT

The **Escalation Decision Card** — before any escalation, fill this out in writing. If you can't complete every field, don't escalate.

```
WHAT'S STUCK  (one sentence, verb + object)
[e.g., "VPC peering to the pilot subnet has not been provisioned."]

WHO IS THE CURRENT OWNER
[Name, function, latest known status of their work]

WHAT DIRECT ATTEMPTS I'VE MADE (dated)
- [date] [channel] [outcome]
- [date] [channel] [outcome]
- [date] [channel] [outcome]
(If fewer than 3 rows and less than 5 business days elapsed, close this card. Not ready.)

WHY IT'S BLOCKED, NOT JUST SLOW
[Specific decision or resource that only exists at a higher level. If you can't name it, close this card.]

COST OF EATING THE DELAY
[Days slipped, milestones affected, dollars if quantifiable]

COST OF THE ESCALATION
[Whose favor am I burning; what's my remaining budget with them; what will they hold against me next quarter]

NET DECISION
[Escalate | Wait | Try one more direct path first]

IF ESCALATING:
  TO WHOM: [Name, title, why them and not someone else]
  THROUGH WHOM: [Champion? Executive sponsor? Direct?]
  ASK: [Specific decision or unblock, one sentence]
  TONE: [Neutral. Never blaming the blocker.]
  FOLLOWUP TO THE BLOCKER: [The message you send them within 4 hours so they aren't ambushed]
```

The last item is the one everyone forgets. Ambushed people become permanent enemies. Any escalation must be accompanied by a same-day note to the person you went over: "wanted to give you a heads-up — I flagged the timeline to [SVP name] since we need a decision by [date]. Not a complaint about you; you've been responsive. Happy to jump on a call to align."

## FIELD NOTES

- The escalation-to-blame-the-blocker instinct is the strongest and the most expensive. Fight it every time.
- Escalate up your own reporting line before you escalate up the customer's. Your engagement lead may have context, a phone number, or an old relationship that unblocks in one call.
- The best escalations look like "asks for a decision," not "complaints about a delay." Executives make decisions faster than they resolve complaints.
- Sometimes escalating *early* is right — but only when the decision itself is time-sensitive (a security review that needs to be fast-tracked because a regulator is asking). Match the escalation shape to the actual constraint.
- After every escalation, follow up with the blocker in person. Coffee if you can. Rebuild the direct channel; you'll need it.
- Track your escalation budget explicitly in your stakeholder map (Lesson 02). Two per year per named human, roughly. When you hit one, note it.
- The champion's escalation budget is not yours. Do not spend theirs without them agreeing on which favor is being burned and why.

## INTERVIEW ANGLE

Escalation judgment is a top-tier behavioral probe — it separates FDEs who understand political economy from those who treat the customer like an infinite queue.

Sample questions:

1. *"IT has been slow on a critical ticket for two weeks. What do you do?"* — Strong answers: check what "slow" means (touched? untouched? who owns it?), exhaust direct paths, only then consider escalation, and always with a same-day heads-up to the blocker. Weak answers escalate immediately.
2. *"Tell me about a time you decided not to escalate something that was blocking you."* — STAR. Best stories weigh the political cost, name what you did instead, and show that eating the delay was the correct call.
3. *"When would you go over your champion's head?"* — Trick question. Rarely, and only after telling them you're going to. Never behind their back.

## DRILL

Recall a real situation where you were blocked (work, personal, anything). Fill out the Escalation Decision Card in full. Be honest about the direct-attempts row — did you actually try three channels, or was it "I emailed once and got frustrated"? Then write both messages side by side: (1) the escalation message to the higher-up, (2) the same-day heads-up to the person you went over. Compare tone. If the second is harder to write than the first, you're not ready to escalate yet.
