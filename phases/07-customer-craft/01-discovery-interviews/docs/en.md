# Discovery Interviews: Questions That Surface the Real Problem

**Phase 7 · Lesson 01 · ~1.5h**

## PROBLEM

The first discovery call at a regional insurer. The VP of Underwriting has forty-five minutes and a lot to say. She opens: "We need AI to help our underwriters make better decisions." The junior FDE nods, takes notes, and asks follow-ups like "what kind of decisions?" and "how would AI help?" Forty-five minutes later he has three pages of notes and a scope so wide it could mean anything from a chatbot to a full replacement of the actuarial team. The senior FDE on the call — who barely spoke — turns off her camera and says: "the real problem is that Karen retires in June and nobody else can price aviation risk without her. That's what we're building." She got it from *one* question: "who's the person everyone escalates the hardest cases to?"

Discovery is not stenography. It is a targeted excavation for the operational reality that the executive framing hides.

## INTUITION

Every customer opens with the *stated* problem — usually a strategy phrase they'd say to their own boss. Under it is the *observed* problem (what's actually painful this week) and under that is the *root cause* (why it keeps happening). Your job in the first hour is to move down that stack, fast, without making anyone defensive.

Three moves do most of the work:

1. **Shift from noun to verb.** "AI to help underwriters" becomes "walk me through the last case where an underwriter got stuck." Concrete over abstract, past over future.
2. **Follow the escalation.** Whoever gets escalated *to* is where the tacit knowledge lives. Whoever escalates *from* is where the pain lives.
3. **Ask what already exists.** Every enterprise has fifteen spreadsheets attempting to solve the problem. Those spreadsheets are your PRD.

Bad discovery is symmetric — you ask questions, they answer, you feel productive. Good discovery is asymmetric — you leave with three artifacts they didn't know they had (a workflow map, a person-of-record list, and a metric everyone secretly tracks).

## BUILD IT

The **Discovery Question Bank**, organized by depth. Print it. Take it into the room.

**Layer 1 — Situation (5 min, warm-up):**
- "Who's on your team? Who owns what?"
- "What does a good week look like for you right now?"
- "What are you being measured on this quarter?"

**Layer 2 — Workflow (15 min, the core):**
- "Walk me through the last time [thing] happened, from start to finish."
- "Who touches it? In what order? What's the handoff?"
- "Where does it live between steps — a system, a spreadsheet, an inbox, someone's head?"
- "How long did that take? How long *should* it take?"

**Layer 3 — Pain (10 min, the reveal):**
- "What's the worst version of this you've seen in the last month?"
- "What did you do this week that you're embarrassed still requires a human?"
- "If [key person] quit tomorrow, what breaks?"
- "What's the workaround everyone knows about but nobody has fixed?"

**Layer 4 — Value (10 min, the money question):**
- "If we made this 10x faster, what would change downstream?"
- "Who else would care? Who would push back?"
- "Is there a number your boss asks you about that this would move?"

**Layer 5 — Constraints (5 min, the reality check):**
- "What's off-limits? Data we can't touch, people we can't talk to?"
- "Who else has tried to fix this? What happened?"
- "What's your appetite: a pilot in six weeks, or a real system in six months?"

Rules: never ask two questions in one breath. Sit through the silence after Layer 3 questions — the useful answer arrives at second seven. Write verbatim quotes; you'll need them for the readout.

## FIELD NOTES

- The person who agreed to the call is rarely the person doing the painful work. Ask "who else should I talk to?" before you leave — that's how you double your surface area.
- Executives will happily talk for 45 minutes about strategy. You must interrupt, politely, to get to workflow. "That's useful context — can we ground it in a recent example?" is a magic phrase.
- Record only with explicit permission, and even then take handwritten notes; the act of writing forces prioritization.
- The most valuable sentence you will hear all week is "well, technically we're not supposed to, but…" — that's a workaround, and workarounds are product opportunities.
- End every discovery call with the same three closes: "who else?", "what would I look at to see this myself — a spreadsheet, a dashboard?", "what would make this a waste of your time three months from now?"

## INTERVIEW ANGLE

Behavioral rounds at Palantir, Anthropic Applied AI, and Sierra will probe discovery skill directly. They want to know you can extract signal from an executive who is themselves confused.

Sample questions:

1. *"A customer VP tells you 'we want AI to improve customer service.' Walk me through your next twenty minutes on that call."* — Tests whether you shift from noun to verb, ask for a recent example, and identify who the escalation target is. Weak candidates start proposing solutions.
2. *"Tell me about a time you realized halfway through a project that you'd been solving the wrong problem."* — STAR probe. They want the moment of discovery failure and what changed in your intake process afterward.
3. *"What's the one question you'd ask if you only got one?"* — There's no single right answer, but strong ones cluster around "walk me through the last time this went wrong" or "who does everyone escalate to?" Weak ones are strategy questions.

## DRILL

Grab a friend who works in an operational role (ops, finance, HR, customer support). Give yourself 30 minutes. Run the five-layer bank on them for a workflow they own. At the end, produce three artifacts on one page: (1) the workflow diagram from Layer 2, (2) the top three pains from Layer 3 with verbatim quotes, (3) the metric-that-would-move from Layer 4. Send it back to them within 24 hours and ask "did I get this right?" Their edits are the real discovery output.
