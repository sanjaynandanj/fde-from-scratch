# The FDE Career Arc: IC Field Work → Engagement Lead → Product Boomerang

Phase 0 · Lesson 05 · ~1h

## PROBLEM

Two FDEs join the same company the same month. Three years later, one has become the person executives request by name — she's led four engagements, turned two field hacks into product features, and just moved to lead a new vertical. The other has done the same year three times: parachute in, grind the integration, burn out a little more, repeat. Same title, same raw talent. The difference is that one treated field work as a *stage* with deliberate exits and compounding assets, and the other treated it as a treadmill. The FDE arc rewards people who know what each stage is for.

## INTUITION

The arc has three broad stages, with well-trodden exits at each:

**Stage 1 — IC in the field (years 0–2).** You are the hands: integrations, prototypes, demos, the 2am pipeline fix before the exec review. What you're actually accumulating: pattern recognition across messy enterprises (every customer thinks their chaos is unique; you learn it's the same six problems), demo craft, and scar tissue about what kills pilots. The trap at this stage: being *only* fast. Speed without writing down what you learned converts to nothing.

**Stage 2 — Engagement lead (years 2–4).** You own the account outcome, not the tasks: scoping the pilot, running the exec relationship, deciding what gets built and what gets declined, unblocking the junior FDEs. The skill shift is from doing to *choosing* — and from being trusted with code to being trusted with revenue. Many FDEs discover here that they've been doing shadow-PM and shadow-sales work all along; this stage makes it explicit. The trap: heroically doing stage-1 work while holding stage-2 responsibility, which caps the team at your personal throughput.

**Stage 3 — the boomerang (years 3+), in one of several directions:**

- **Product boomerang** (the canonical one, institutionalized at Palantir): you've seen the same field problem five times, so you move to the product org and build the generalized version. Field FDEs make unusually good product engineers/PMs because their roadmap intuitions come from watching real users fail.
- **GTM/leadership boomerang**: vertical lead, head of forward deployment, or founding the deployment function at a younger company.
- **Founder exit**: FDE work is the best founder training available short of founding — you've done discovery, sales support, delivery, and support for someone else's product. A visible cohort of enterprise-AI startups is founded by ex-forward-deployed people who productized a pain they kept re-solving.

The compounding asset across all stages is the same: **a library of problems you've seen resolved end-to-end.** Every phase of this curriculum is a synthetic version of that library.

## MAP IT

1. Sketch the three stages as columns. Under each, write: what you produce, what you accumulate, and the trap. Do it from memory, then check against the text.
2. For each of the three boomerang directions, name one public example you can find (company blog posts, founder bios, LinkedIn paths of forward-deployed alumni). Verifying the arc is real makes it concrete.
3. Write your own target: which stage-3 direction do you want, and therefore what should you *deliberately collect* during stage 1? (Product boomerang → collect generalizable feature gaps. Founder → collect unsolved pains and buyer contacts. Leadership → collect playbooks.)

## FIELD NOTES

- Stage lengths vary hugely by company. Fast-growing teams promote to engagement lead in a year; mature ones take three. Deal flow, not calendar time, drives growth — one FDE year with three engagements teaches more than two years parked on one account.
- Burnout is the real occupational hazard, and it clusters at late stage 1: travel, context-switching, and hero culture. Sustainable FDEs ruthlessly protect build days and treat handoff (making yourself unnecessary — Phase 1) as a promotion strategy, not a loss of importance.
- The boomerang requires *writing things down*. The FDE who files field-gap reports and internal memos gets pulled into product; the one who only ships doesn't, no matter how good.

## INTERVIEW ANGLE

Career-arc questions test whether you'll stay long enough to be worth training and whether you understand the role's leverage.

Sample questions:

1. "Where do you want to be in three years?" (Strong answer names a stage-3 direction and connects it to the field library you'd build; weak answer treats FDE as a stepping stone *away* from customers.)
2. "What would make you leave this role?" (Honest, non-flaky answers: stagnant deal flow, no path to engagement ownership.)
3. "Tell me about a time you turned a one-off solution into something reusable." (The boomerang instinct, tested behaviorally — have a story where you generalized.)

## DRILL

Interview one person (or, failing access, read two detailed accounts — engineering blogs and podcast transcripts from ex-Palantir/lab forward-deployed folks are plentiful) about their forward-deployed career. Map their path onto the three stages: where were the transitions, what triggered them, what did they collect? Write a half-page comparison against the model in this lesson — including where reality contradicted it.
